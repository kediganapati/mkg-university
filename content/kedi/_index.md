## Identity & Character

**Kiran** — My birth name, given by my mother during the Namkaran ceremony. It means a ray of light — a shining beam that illuminates darkness. It was the name I carried into the world, before the world revealed to me who I truly was.

**Kedi** — The name I consciously chose in 2016, when I understood my true identity and mission. "Kedi" represents a complex and fearless personality — one who carries both divine and demonic attributes within. It symbolizes a man who is free from shackles: a rule-maker, a patriot, a donor, a dark horse, a strategist, a rebel, and a seeker of truth.

**Kedi Ganapati** — My official documents carry the name Kedi Kailas Khot. In 2016, I experienced a moment of deep self-realisation — I understood that my life was not ordinary. From that day, I committed to research, reinterpret, and rewrite the foundational scriptures of Sanatan Dharma, and to initiate its renaissance for the modern age.

**Kedi Character** — Don't define my position or character by thinking I am only a sage. When I was a student, I was not a banker. When I was a sales executive, I was not a massage therapist. When I was a detective agent, I was not a hairdresser. When I was a boxer, I was not a joker. When I was a blogger, I was not an author. And now, when I am a sage, I do not know what I will become next.

More importantly, don't argue with others about my one particular character. Maybe you have not seen that side of my character, but someone else has deeply experienced it.

## Family Background

I was born into a modest Maharashtrian family. My ancestors carried the Brahmin surname "Khot," though over generations the family diversified into farming and service professions.

My mother, Kamal, was a retired Anganwadi worker, and my father, Kailas, passed away in 2001. My younger brother, Sachin, is an employee. My paternal grandfather worked in a Mumbai textile mill, while my maternal grandfather worked as a fitter in the BEST government transport service.

My early life was deeply shaped by instability, poverty, and emotional separation. I have remained closely connected with my mother's family, while having little connection with my father's side. Today, I live with my mother and younger brother in Navi Mumbai.

## Childhood: The Orphanage Years

I was born on 2 July 1989, in Ghatkopar, Mumbai. My native village is Aswalewadi, 32 Shirala, Sangli, Maharashtra.

Due to poverty and family crisis, at the age of 5, I was sent to an orphanage in Pune for seven years. Those years were harsh. I was mischievous, rebellious, and unwilling to tolerate injustice. I was frequently punished. The orphanage administration even attempted to expel me twice, but my mother, unable to raise me at that time, requested that I be allowed to stay.

At age 11, I was told that a foster family wanted to adopt me. Later I realised they were seeking labor, not a son. That moment shattered me — but it also awakened something powerful inside. I took a silent vow:

*"Today, no one accepts me. One day, I will give the world such a gift that it will accept me — not as a servant, but as a force."*

At age 12, I began visiting a Ram temple at 5 AM for Kakad Aarti. Initially, I went for prasad. But soon the questions arose: Who is God? What is Dharma? What is existence? At that age, without guidance, I decided I would become a researcher of God.

## Bharat Tirtha Yatra

Since 2016, I have undertaken extensive solo bike pilgrimages across India on my Hero Splendour (2006 model). Traveling thousands of kilometers has been my living university.

**Bharat Tirtha Yatra — Bike Travel Timeline**

- Rameshwaram
- Kashi Vishwanath
- Kamakhya
- Somnath
- Dwarkadhish
- Jagannath Puri
- Kailash Temple
- Khajuraho
- Ashtavinayak
- Jyotirlingas
- Kumbh Mela Prayagraj
- Arunachal Pradesh


In 2012, I was working in a field job at Shriram Finance. The company required me to get a bike. No bike, no job. So I bought a second-hand bike to keep my job.

In 2016, I started travelling all over India with the same 2006 Hero Splendour. The condition of the bike was not good. The bike broke down on almost every trip. Still, I used the same bike for every trip because somehow every trip became successful.

From 2016 to 2020, I was mainly travelling from one state to another state by bike. I was not going to temples. From 2021 to 2025, I started travelling from one temple to another temple to collect evidence and understand Sanatan Dharma.

After giving money for household expenses, I usually had around ₹10,000 left. I had three options: spend ₹5,000 on shopping, spend ₹5,000 on bike repair, or put the money into petrol and travel across India. I used to spend around ₹1,000 on shopping, around ₹1,000 on bike servicing, and the remaining money on petrol.

I did not have money for hotels, so I used to sleep at petrol pumps. In the morning, I used to take bath in the petrol pump toilet and start my journey again.

Whenever a client gave me a tip, I did not see that money as money. I used to see petrol for my bike trip. If I got ₹500 extra, I used to see ₹500 as petrol for 300 km of travel.

My 2006 Hero Splendour was not just my bike. It became my travel companion. India became my classroom, and travelling became my living university.

My mother always says that my bike is my wife because without my bike I never travel anywhere. My bike always takes me wherever I want to go and brings me back home safely.

My bike's wheel rims were bent, the handle was bent, the drum brake was worn out, the battery was damaged, the headlight was bent, the suspension was damaged, the speedometer was not working, and there were no indicators. The chain and bearings used to break on almost every trip.

During my journeys, I gave lifts to around 1,000 people. The problem was that whenever someone asked me for a lift, I could not say no. Sometimes I had to say no when people asked for a lift because the bike chain had become so loose that it used to come off every 200 metres. And honestly, much of it was my own mistake. I used to ride 1,000 km nonstop and forget to do basic servicing and chain tightening.

During one trip, the chassis broke from underneath at 60 km/h — the bike nearly split in two. That day I promised myself: no lifts until I bought a new bike. But every time I hit the road, I forgot that promise.  I love riding my bike, and I love giving people lifts during my journey.
